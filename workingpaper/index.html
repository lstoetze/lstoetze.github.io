  1. Coal Rush: The Built Legacy of the Industrial Revolution and the Rise of the Radical Right
    Nils Blossey, Lukas Haffert, and Lukas F Stoetzer
    2025
    Populism & Radical Right
    Abs Bib Scholar

    Historical industrial centers have shifted to the right but have done so at different speeds and intensities. We argue that this variation can be explained by differences in the historical industrialization process. Communities that industrialized later and more intensively realign more toward the radical right today. This is because the built environment shaped by the original industrialization drives demographic persistence and neighborhood disadvantage. To examine our argument, we study the effects of nineteenth-century coal mining in Germany’s Ruhr area. We match the geolocation of over 1,000 mining shafts, historical plant-level employment data, and the spread of company housing with contemporary electoral results at the neighborhood level. For identification, we exploit the depth of coal deposits that governed the adoption of deep-shaft mining. The findings demonstrate how the path of economic development influences voting in the long run.

  2. Emphasizing Owned Issues or Riding the Wave? Experimental Evidence on the Vote Effects of Issue Emphasis
    Lukas F Stoetzer, Heike Klüver, and Petra Schleiter
    American European Political Science Association Conference 2022. 2022
    Voting DecisionsParty Competition
    Abs Bib Scholar

    The selective emphasis of political issues is a key strategy for winning electoral support during campaigns. We present experimental evidence on the effects of two prominent issue emphasis strategies: an issue ownership strategy, which emphasizes parties’ perceived competence advantages, and a “riding the wave” strategy, which focuses on issues currently salient to voters. Using panel survey data from the 2021 German federal election, we show that emphasizing the Christian Democrats’ perceived competence on the economy increased vote intention, while emphasizing the salient issue of the environment produced smaller and more imprecisely estimated effects. Differences between the two strategies are not statistically distinguishable in our experiment, but the results highlight the potential of ownership-based emphasis to increase vote intention.

  1. Breaking Pre-electoral Coalition Commitments: Electoral Accountability and Satisfaction with Democracy
    Lukas F. Stoetzer, and Oke Bahnsen
    2026
    Coalition PoliticsRepresentation
    Abs Scholar

    Pre-electoral coalition signals are widely seen as essential for democratic accountability in proportional representation systems, as they help voters anticipate post-electoral governments. Among these, pre-electoral coalition commitments to enter or rule out specific coalitions provide particularly strong guidance to voters. However, we know little about how citizens react when parties violate such commitments. We argue that while electoral punishment for breaking coalition commitments is context-dependent, such violations reduce satisfaction with democracy more broadly. We test this claim using a quasi-experimental analysis of the 1996 New Zealand election and a survey experiment conducted during the 2020 Irish government formation process. We find that violations of coalition commitments consistently reduce democratic satisfaction, whereas electoral punishment of the violating party appears only in the survey experiment and depends on voters’ preferences over the coalition outcome. These results show how violations of informal institutional commitments can undermine democratic support even in the absence of strong electoral sanctions.

  1. How to Break Populist Parties’ Appeal? An Experimental Evaluation of Communication Counter-Strategies
    Heike Klüver, Ferdinand Geissler, Felix Hartmann, Johannes Giesecke, Lukas F. Stoetzer, and Petra Schleiter
    2024
    Populism & Radical RightParty Competition
    Abs Scholar

    Right-wing populist parties have been on the rise across Europe, but there is limited evidence on how mainstream parties can effectively counter this appeal. This study experimentally evaluates four communication counter-strategies—highlighting mainstream parties’ representation of citizen interests, emphasizing their performance, exposing populists’ self-serving motivations, and promoting alternative identities. We fielded a large-scale survey experiment among over 24,000 respondents in Germany using real-world messages drawn from social media, party manifestos, and press releases. The results indicate that exposing populist parties as self-serving is an effective counter-strategy, especially among voters initially supportive of right-wing populists, while other strategies show more limited effects. These findings contribute to understanding how political communication can mitigate the appeal of populist parties.

  2. Attribute Relevance in Conjoint Analysis
    Lukas F. Stoetzer, and Asya Magazinnik
    2026
    Methods
    Abs Scholar

    RResearchers often rely on conjoint analysis to understand which attributes drive respondents’ decisionmaking, yet standard estimands are not designed to measure attribute relevance. We introduce a novel approach to measuring attribute relevance in conjoint analysis by drawing on causal attribution methods. Our proposed estimand, the average probability of necessary and sufficient causation (APNS), captures the probability that swapping two profiles’ levels on an attribute would change a respondent’s choice. We establish nonparametric identification under assumptions related to preference heterogeneity, and provide simple difference-in-means estimators that can be applied to existing conjoint data structures. Two empirical applications illustrate the method. A candidate choice experiment in Germany reveals significant preference heterogeneity that leads standard estimands to mask relevance, while under the right assumptions the APNS recovers it. By contrast, in a replication of a prominent refugee experiment, we find that assuming preference homogeneity is unproblematic for many attributes of asylum-seekers.

  3. The Effect of Political Arguments on Voting Decisions
    Lukas F Stoetzer, and Denise Traber
    Swiss Political Science Association (SPSA) Annual Conference in Zurich 2019 and Midwest Political Science Association conference in Chicago 2019. 2019
    Voting Decisions
    Abs Bib Scholar

    The question of what role political arguments play in democracy has taken centre stage in times of populism, post-truth discourse, and identity politics. It is relevant to analyse how political arguments affect policy preferences, but also how these effects carry over to the election of representatives. In this article, we test the persuasive effect of policy arguments on voting decisions. We combine a persuasion experiment using policy arguments with a conjoint experiment, in which voters choose between two candidates who differ in various policy positions. We conduct the experiment with two representative samples of the German voting-age population in June 2021 and August 2022 before the Election. In addition to testing the downstream effects of arguments on candidate choice, we distinguish between a persuasive effect of policy arguments (i.e. they affect vote decisions by changing the direction of policy preferences) and a priming effect of arguments (i.e. they affect vote decisions by changing the salience of policy preferences). The findings show that persuasion plays a more central role in how arguments shape voters’ decision making. The results have a multitude of implications for the role of political arguments in Democracies, political campaigns, and party competition.

  4. When Protest Enters Public Space: The Effects of Direct Exposure on Bystanders
    Violeta Haas, Tim Wappenhans, Ferdinand Geißler, Felix Hartmann, Daniel Bischof, Johannes Giesecke, Macartan Humphreys, Heike Klüver, and Lukas F. Stoetzer
    2025
    Abs Scholar

    Can protests influence bystanders? %citizens who merely encounter them? Existing studies show that protest events affect public opinion and political behavior, but cannot separate direct encounters from media coverage, social diffusion, and subsequent mobilization. We isolate this effect in a preregistered field experiment conducted around three protests organized by the climate movement Fridays for Future in Berlin, Germany. Pedestrians were randomly routed either into close proximity to an ongoing protest (treatment) or away from (control) protest activity, creating incidental exposure to collective action in its natural setting. We find little evidence that direct encounters substantially change attitudes, perceived social norms, or party support. Yet exposed participants donate significantly more to an environmental cause, particularly when already sympathetic to it. Our findings suggest that the broader political consequences of protest may depend more on amplification and repeated exposure than on one-off persuasion, while direct encounters can translate existing political sympathy into immediate material support.

  5. Visual presentation raises trust in election polls
    Elias Koch, Simon Munzert, Linus Hagemann, Hannah Rajski, Thomas Gschwend, Cornelius Erfort, and Lukas F. Stoetzer
    2026
    Election Forecasting
    Abs Scholar

    Opinion polls are central to voters’ understanding of electoral competition, shaping expectations, strategic choices, and turnout. But how the news media present polls varies widely, and whether that presentation shapes public perception is largely unknown. We conducted a visual conjoint experiment with 23,444 participants during the 2025 German federal election campaign, randomly varying the visual format and technical content of the same poll report across 160 versions. Visual formats, including bar charts, dot plots, and line graphs, were consistently perceived as more appealing and trustworthy than text-only reports. The trust gains were largest among participants with lower data and visualization literacy and less accurate prior beliefs about the poll standings, whereas the appeal of visualization was greatest among the most literate. Technical features mattered less and not always favorably: disclosing statistical uncertainty reduced the perceived attractiveness of a report, while reference values shifted trust depending on the benchmark used. These findings suggest that visual reporting holds consistent advantages over the text-only format that still dominates poll reporting in the news media, particularly for audiences who struggle most to interpret quantitative information.

  6. Stacking Election Forecasts
    Lukas F. Stoetzer, and Cornelius Erfort
    2026
    Election ForecastingMethods
    Abs Scholar

    Accurately predicting election outcomes is challenging due to model uncertainty, limited data availability, and the sequential nature of electoral prediction. While election forecasting increasingly relies on diverse models that combine different theories, predictors, and data sources, forecasts are still commonly treated as a competition among individual models rather than combined into ensemble forecasts. We propose the use of stacking weights, computed from leave-future-out predictive performance, to systematically combine forecasts from different election forecasting models. Our framework accounts for model uncertainty, the complete predictive distribution and weights forecasts according to their past predictive performance. We demonstrate the approach using applications to U.S. presidential elections and German federal elections. The results show that stacked forecasts improve on individual and alternative ensemble models in terms of predictive density of the realized outcomes.

  1. Precision-Weighted Aggregation of Citizen Election Forecasts
    Lukas F. Stoetzer, and Hannah Rajski
    2026
    Election ForecastingMethods
    Abs Scholar

    Citizen forecasts aggregate individuals’ electoral expectations to predict election outcomes, typically weighting all respondents equally and implicitly assuming homogeneous forecast quality. Yet forecasting accuracy varies across individuals, suggesting that weighting more capable forecasters more heavily could improve performance. Existing weighting approaches, however, rely on information about forecasters, such as political knowledge or past accuracy, and therefore require historical data. We propose an elicitation-based approach that weights forecasts by respondents’ subjective precision. We derive oracle weights that minimize the mean squared error of weighted vote-share forecasts and show that a set of feasible precision-based weights can approximate them. To estimate precision directly, we introduce Manski-style elicitation questions that collect both expected vote shares and subjective uncertainty assessments. Using original pre-election survey data from approximately 14,600 eligible voters in the 2025 German federal election, we forecast seven parties’ vote shares nationally and across 299 electoral districts. Nationally, precision weighting reduces RMSE from 1.39 to 0.95 percentage points. In district-level forecasts, additional corrections for overconfidence yield the largest improvements, although gains are more modest.

  2. Coordination Spirals in Proportional Elections
    Lukas F. Stoetzer, Elias Koch, and Thomas Gschwend
    2026
    Coalition PoliticsRepresentation
    Abs Scholar

    Fragmentation in proportional representation (PR) systems is often seen as a source of difficulties for government formation and electoral accountability. What can hold these party systems together? We argue that PR also contains an inherent coordination mechanism that concentrates competition on the two leading parties. When polls signal a close race at the top, and both parties hold balanced potentials in the electorate, voters shift support away from smaller parties toward the frontrunners, thereby reducing party system fragmentation. We show formally that when voters derive utility from which party wins, equilibrium behavior exhibits a coordination spiral in close contests: strategic voting feeds back into greater concentration on the top two. We test this argument in two studies. First, linking pre-election polls to outcomes across 320 PR elections, we find that the combined top-two vote share rises by up to 10 percentage points in close races, with effects emerging roughly two to four weeks before election day. Second, panel data from the 2021 German campaign — a close and balanced race — show that the same dynamic operates at the individual level. Together, these findings reveal a previously underappreciated stabilizing force that arises endogenously from electoral competition in proportional systems.
